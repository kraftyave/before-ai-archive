The Before Times

Browse the collection

Before the machines learned to think, humans made things with their hands. They wrote letters on paper, wound their watches, and got lost without asking for directions.

This is an archive of what we almost lost. A curatorial act of remembering.

— Curatorial Statement, 2026

Exhibits

Selected artifacts from the pre-digital era, each carrying the weight of human intention.

Exhibit No. 001
c. 2400 BCE — Present

Handwritten Letters

Correspondence carried across continents by ship, train, and foot. Each stroke of ink carried the weight of intention, the particular slant of a person's hand.

"A letter always arrives too early or too late." — Karl Kraus

Exhibit No. 002
1963 — 2000s

The Mixtape

A curated sequence of songs recorded onto magnetic tape. An act of love, a declaration of taste. The perfect playlist required patience, precision, and perfect timing on the pause button.

Each tape was a fingerprint of someone's emotional landscape.

Exhibit No. 003
1948 — Present

Polaroid Photographs

An image that developed before your eyes. Impermanent, imperfect, irreplaceable. You could shake it to make it dry faster, though that was always a myth.

No filters. No retakes. Just a single frame of captured light.

Exhibit No. 004
Antiquity — Present

Recipe Cards

Handwritten on index cards, stained with butter and chocolate. Measurements were approximate, instructions assumed knowledge. The cook knew more than the recipe told.

"A pinch of this, a handful of that. You'll know when it's right."

Exhibit No. 005
Prehistory — 2010s

Paper Maps

Large sheets of folded paper that demanded you unfold your entire world to see where you were. You could draw on them, lose them, and discover places you never meant to find.

Getting lost was part of the journey. There was no recalculating.

Exhibit No. 006
1888 — 2010s

Film Photography

Twenty-four or thirty-six frames per roll. Every click cost money and counted. You composed carefully, waited for the light, and hoped the lab got it right.

The anticipation of the developed roll is a lost form of suspense.

Exhibit No. 007
c. 600 CE — Present

The Private Diary

Pages filled with uncensored thought, sealed with a tiny lock. The only reader was supposed to be yourself. A conversation with no audience but time.

"The diary is the only place where you can speak the truth." — Anais Nin

Featured Exhibit

A deeper look at one artifact from the collection.

c. 1700 BCE — 2000s

Love Letters

Before texting, before DMs, before swiping right, there was the love letter. Written on aerogrammes, hidden in desks, pressed between books. The writer chose every word knowing it could not be unsent.

Letters took days or weeks to arrive. Absence was measured in mail deliveries, not notification badges. The weight of paper carried the weight of feeling.

"I wrote to you every day for a year. You kept every one."

What else will we forget to remember?
